## Overview

The creator of this corpus once ran a fully loaded productivity system: a task manager with a dozen projects, a calendar colour-coded by energy level, three apps for notes, a weekly review ritual, and a stack of to-do software that had arrived with the promise of finally fixing everything. It produced everything except results. Then came one "not today" day — no system, no apps, one piece of paper — and he dropped the machine and doubled his output in a single afternoon.

The glitch is real, and it deserves a precise name: the more you add — tools, tasks, systems, rituals — the less that gets done. More system, less output.

> The glitch was never the tools. The glitch is you.

This is not a confession and not a personality flaw. It is structural, it is known to science, and knowing it is half the fix.

## 01 — The Addition Bias

In 2021, Adams, Converse, Hales and Klotz published a study in Nature that runs across eight experiments: when people are asked to improve a system, they default to adding elements — even when subtracting would serve them better. Shown a structure with a flaw on one side, participants added components to the other side rather than removing the flawed one; asked to improve an essay, they added sentences instead of cutting them; asked to design a better itinerary, they added stops. The bias survived incentives: people chose addition even when subtraction was explicitly rewarded.

The finding matters because it reclassifies your cluttered desk. The problem was never your discipline. The structural default of the human mind is to improve by adding, and Klotz's *Subtract* — the practitioner translation of this science — argues that subtraction is the highest-leverage improvement available to you, precisely because almost nobody reaches for it. The bias is not personal; it is inherited, and it runs on you as reliably as it runs on everyone else. Knowing that is the beginning of working against it.

## 02 — Cognitive Load Is the Tax

Every tool, toggle, tab and ritual you maintain charges attention rent. The cost is invisible because it is paid in dribs: the app that needs updating, the folder that needs filing, the review that must be performed, the decision about where a note goes. Each is small; together they are a standing levy on the attention filter of Chapter 1, which spends its finite budget on housekeeping instead of on the work itself. This is the mechanical reason the loaded system produced nothing: the system was consuming the very resource it was meant to protect.

The One-Task Rule of Chapter 25 is the load-reduction surgery. A single task, written once, executed once — no dashboards to consult, no rituals to perform, no housekeeping. Subtraction is not a mood; it is a tax cut.

## 03 — The Subtraction Audit

Once a quarter, run the audit. List every tool, commitment, task and recurring meeting you currently hold. For each one, ask a single question: what would happen if this disappeared? The answer sorts everything into two piles. The indispensable survive — a tool whose absence would break a process you actually want. Everything else is deleted or shrunk: the meeting becomes an email, the app becomes an uninstall, the ritual becomes an occasional.

You have met this move before. The audit is the Equation Check of Chapter 2 run at the scale of a whole system — the same question, what does this actually buy me, asked of everything at once. And it comes with a standing principle: subtract first. Before you add anything — a tool, a habit, a commitment — remove one thing. The queue stays at its size, and the system only ever gets leaner.

## 04 — The Glitch in Practice

Run one unsystematic day a week: the "not today" day. No task manager, no app, no colour-coded calendar. One paper list. One task at a time, in the winner's loop of Chapter 25. The day is a controlled experiment in subtraction, and it produces an evidence base your dashboard never could: which tools actually carry weight, and which carry prestige. The app you never open, the ritual you perform for show, the system you maintain because maintaining it feels productive — a not-today day makes each one visible.

Most people discover that their effective load is a fraction of their installed load. The instruments are not the music. On one paper list, one task at a time, you can hear the difference.

## 05 — The Counter-Temptation

There is a dangerous moment in every subtraction: the moment the lean system works. The bias, which never sleeps, immediately proposes an addition — a new app, a new ritual, a new optimisation of something just optimised. Success bloat is the glitch's second act; it does not need your failure, only your momentum.

So subtraction must be institutionalised, not improvised. The quarterly audit is non-negotiable — a calendar appointment like any other. And the standing rule is written where you will see it daily: add one, remove one. The system stays at its size by design. The glitch is structural, and the defence against it must be structural too.

## Key Ideas

- The addition bias is structural: humans improve systems by adding, even when subtracting is better.
- Every tool charges attention rent; cognitive load is the tax.
- The subtraction audit asks one question: what would happen if this disappeared?
- The audit is the Equation Check run at system scale.
- Subtract first: never add anything without removing something.
- A "not today" day reveals which tools carry weight and which carry prestige.

## Apply Today

1. Run a 30-minute mini-audit tonight: list every tool, commitment and recurring meeting you hold.
2. For each item, answer the audit question: what would happen if this disappeared?
3. Delete or archive one unused tool — today, not "eventually".
4. Schedule one not-today day this week: paper list, one task at a time, no task manager.
5. Write "add one, remove one" where you will see it every day.

## The Science

Adams et al. (2021) ran eight experiments in which participants improved systems — structures, essays, itineraries — and defaulted to adding elements even when subtracting was better, with the bias persisting under explicit incentives to subtract. This is one of the most robust modern findings in the corpus. It licenses the conclusion that your instinct to add is a default, not a judgment. It does not license the claim that every addition is wrong — only that subtraction is systematically underused and should be consciously reached for first.

Klotz's *Subtract* (2021) is a practitioner's framework rather than a study — it translates the bias research into a method for living: seek subtraction before addition, make taking away the first instinct. As a framework it is coherent and faithful to the science it builds on; as with any framework, its value depends on whether you run it. The audit in this chapter is that framework with a calendar attached.
